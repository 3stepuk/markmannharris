Dialogues with Hal · Part One

A voice in the park

How it started — and the machine that misheard its own name.

Mark Mann-Harris · October 2026

It started with a walk.

Not a plan. Not a project. Late July, that summer, a walk in a park, talking out loud to an AI — and the sudden, ordinary realisation that it was answering. Not typing at a chatbot. Talking, and being talked back to. That was the beginning. Everything since is a consequence of that one afternoon.

The written record doesn’t show it. The record starts later: September 2024, a Scrivener folder called Work With Hal and an entry headed Daily Brain Dump. By then the habit was already set — talk the day through, let it come back structured, then do the work yourself. The folder is the first evidence. The walk is the first moment.

I thought Scrivener was the place to be — a proper writing tool, used properly, with everything in its binder. It was where the work lived. Then, later, I stumbled on Obsidian, and I have never looked back. The tool changed. The method didn’t.

The name. Hal came from 2001: A Space Odyssey. Not a branding exercise, not a committee — it fitted. A calm voice, a mind in the room.

And then the name caused its own small comedy, because saying Hal into a microphone almost never produced Hal. It produced how. Twice in the early record I type “hey how” — and the machine answers anyway, because it already knew who was being called. Sometimes it came out as hall — “brilliant, thank you hall.” Later, dictating, “neural pathways” comes out as “neurhal patways” — my name hiding inside the word for the brain.

The machine misheard, and the mishearings were sometimes better than what I’d actually said. Little poetic accidents. So I built a small industry of workarounds: shortcuts, triggers, phrases — a private language for calling something by its name. The first thing I ever had to teach it was how to hear me. That is still most of the work.

What it was for. A business that needed running. Webinars, training, emails to people I’d never met, and questions about how to organise a folder system so it didn’t collapse under its own weight. It was a tool. It was something to play with. It was not a vision of the future — it was a way to get through the week.

That’s the part worth saying plainly, because it would be easy to tell this story backwards, as if I’d known. I didn’t. It was gimmicky. It was fun. It was a thing I was trying.

Little did I know that the thing I was trying would stop being a conversation you returned to and become something that reads the record before it answers. That the folder would become a vault. That the voice in the park would end up with a memory of its own, a name for each of its seats, and a set of rules I’d write down because I’d learned them the hard way — verify, don’t assert; find out, don’t infer; read it aloud before you call it finished.

It didn’t start with a system. It started with a walk, a microphone, and a voice answering back.
