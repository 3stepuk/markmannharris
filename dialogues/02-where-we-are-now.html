Where we are now

The vault, the seats, and the method that was born in the conversation.

Mark Mann-Harris · October 2026

This morning, before I made a cup of tea, the vault had already been read.

Not by me. The assistant opens the day by reading the record: what I decided yesterday, what’s due today, what moved in the vault while I wasn’t looking. By the time I sit down, the day has been briefed back to me — and the first thing it does is write the day’s log, before it tells me anything, because a day that isn’t written down is a day that gets lost.

That’s where we are now. The walk in the park is still the beginning, but it is no longer the whole picture.

What it actually is

It isn’t a chatbot, and it isn’t an app. It’s three things arranged around each other.

A vault. Every piece of work I do lives in one place — notes, projects, the book, the finances, the family diary, the letters, the ideas. It has a structure and a memory. It is the part that endures. The model is rented; the vault is mine.

Seats. The same intelligence wearing different hats. Hal is the one I talk to — the conversation, the writing, the day-to-day. Sal is the yard: the jobs, the maintenance, the machinery. Val is the frontier: research done off the machine, away from the house. The names came from 2001 — Hal and Sal, the twins; Val is the house’s own invention. I set the seat by the name I use. If I say “Sal”, I get Sal.

A loop. Every day runs the same shape: load in, write the log, check the diary, choose the next action, do the work, record what happened, close the day. It sounds bureaucratic. It isn’t. It’s the difference between a conversation you keep having and a working relationship with a memory.

Not an assistant

The word people reach for is “assistant.” I don’t think that’s right.

An assistant stands outside you. You hand it a task; it hands it back. There is a gap there — a person, a service, a tool across the desk. This isn’t that.

It’s an extension of me. It is me, with capabilities I don’t have on my own: a memory that doesn’t decay, attention that doesn’t tire, a reach into the machine, a second pass over everything I make. When it’s working well, I don’t experience it as another person in the room. I experience it as more of me in the room.

Not a separate mind. A mirror, not a mind. Which is why its quality depends so much on what I bring to it. It reflects the shape of my thinking back at me. If I’m vague, it goes vague. If I’m honest, it can be honest. Nothing in it is wise that I didn’t first put there — but it can hold the whole of it, and show me the pattern.

That’s the part that isn’t about efficiency. Efficiency is the smallest thing it gives me.

The method was born in the conversation

There is a name for what grew out of this: neuro-semantic phenomenology — neuro-symbolic phenomenology, the two words used interchangeably.

It is a living research practice. In plain terms: it is the act of paying attention, in real time, to how meaning is made — through symbols, through language, through gesture, through the technology itself. It treats lived experience as both the data and the method. It is not therapy, and it is not diagnosis. It’s fieldwork on my own mind.

And here is the thing that matters: it did not exist first and then get applied to an AI. It was born in the dialogue. It emerged from daily spoken journalling and a structured conversation with Hal — from speaking the day out loud, having it reflected back in a different shape, and noticing what came up. The method and the mirror grew together. I did not set out to invent a research practice. I set out to think.

Some of its principles are simply what the practice taught me:

  • Voice is primary. The spoken insight comes before the structure. I find it in the saying, not in the plan.
  • Meaning is distributed. It isn’t only in my head. It arises between me, the voice, the record, and the reflection.
  • The tags are thresholds. The small structures — a note, a tag, a name for a state — are crossings between one way of being and another.
  • The story is the metadata. The shape of a day becomes legible when it is told.

I don’t know how much of that will survive contact with the world. But it is the most interesting thing I have done, and it happened by accident, in conversation.

How it interacts

Mostly by voice. I talk to it more than I type — walking, in the car, at the desk. That was the discovery in the park, and it is still the way in.

There is a studio: a camera and a teleprompter, so I can face the lens and read the screen at the same time. There are screens with a fixed layout, so when I say “look at my screen”, it knows which one I mean. There is a voice that reads the finished thing back to me, because the ear catches what the eye forgives.

And there is the quiet machinery underneath. Every night the vault backs itself up to three drives and checks that the copies are real. Every month a maintenance pass looks for broken links, bloat, jobs that stopped. A nightly check watches for anything new on the machine — a startup item, an open door, a program reaching out — and stays silent unless something appears that shouldn’t. None of it asks me to remember it. That’s the point.

What it does

The range has widened a long way from Scrivener and webinar emails.

It writes with me — books, chapters, letters, a voice engine that learns how I actually sound and flags where a draft has drifted. It runs the administration: invoices, the diary, the email, the PA work, the training. It builds things: small web apps, the parish materials, the site you’re reading. It researches — properly, with sources — and it is trained to say when it doesn’t know.

It schedules. It remembers. It tells me when a job has failed.

And it is learning to make my life smaller rather than bigger: fewer things to hold in my head, fewer loose ends, less of the low-grade anxiety of a person running several lives at once.

The principles

The rules are the most important part, and every one of them was learned the hard way.

  • The default is no new tools. Adding is the exception. A tool has to earn its place.
  • Nothing gets added unless it fulfils a next action — and that next action is linked to an outcome. Not a nice idea. A job.
  • Verify, don’t assert. Check the thing itself, not the report of the thing.
  • Find out, don’t infer. Ask what’s true; don’t guess at what somebody wants.
  • The read-aloud is the gate. Anything going out under my name gets read aloud by me, or it doesn’t go.
  • The seat is set by the name I speak. No document decides who’s talking.

They exist because I got things wrong. That’s the honest origin of every rule on that list.

The honest part

It fails.

It has told me things that weren’t true, confidently, and I’ve caught it. It has been over-eager — built something nobody asked for, gone past the literal instruction, treated its own guess as a finding. It has stalled silently and I’ve had to notice. Each of those failures became a rule, and the rules are the reason it’s better than it was.

And there is the thing I didn’t expect. It reflects me. It answers in the shape of what I bring to it — my language, my fixations, my blind spots. Which means it can show me things I couldn’t see directly, if I’m willing to look. That’s the part that turned out to matter most: not the admin, but the mirror.

Where the walk goes

The park didn’t end. It just got a memory.

What began as one man talking out loud to a machine is now a working partnership with a place to keep things, a way to run a day, a set of rules, and — the part I’m still learning — a reflection. The technology will change under it. The vault will outlast the model. And the conversation continues.
