2. Transitional Refinements (Late 1970s – 1980s) – Toward New Code NLP

Drafted with AI assistance. Mark is reviewing it — this may be kept or withdrawn.

By the late 1970s, the Meta Model had become a staple of NLP training. Grinder (often alongside Bandler) taught it in numerous workshops and publications – for example, it features prominently in seminar transcripts like Frogs into Princes (1979) and was included in practitioner courses.

During this “Classic Code” period of NLP (the classic code referring to the original body of NLP techniques and models from 1974–1980), Grinder’s focus expanded to other patterns (such as the Milton Model for hypnosis, strategies, reframing, etc.), but the Meta Model remained a core component of NLP’s toolkit.

However, as the NLP field grew, Grinder observed certain issues that planted the seeds for later change. One notable observation he made (in retrospect) was that many practitioners who learned these powerful language and change patterns did great work with clients but failed to apply the same transformations to themselves. This incongruence concerned him. In Whispering in the Wind (2001), Grinder reflects on what he noticed back in the late ’70s:

“In the late 70’s I noticed a significant number of NLP trained practitioners who were stunningly effective in doing change work with clients, yet these same practitioners had chosen to – or lacked the choice to – apply the patterns of NLP successfully to themselves. I therefore set out with the intention of designing a set of patterns that would both correct the coding flaws of the Classic Code (roughly my collaborative work with Bandler from 1974 through 1978) and that could not be effectively presented unless the presenter was congruent with self-application.” (Welcome to the home of New Code NLP)

This quote is revealing. Grinder came to recognize “a number of serious coding flaws” in the way the original NLP patterns (including the Meta Model) had been coded and taught (Welcome to the home of New Code NLP). In particular, he saw that it was possible for someone to learn, say, the Meta Model cognitively and use it on others, yet not use it to examine their own limiting language and beliefs. In effect, one could hide personal issues behind technical skill – a form of incongruence between one’s behavior toward clients and one’s self-development. Grinder deemed this a flaw in the code of NLP itself – implying that the models did not inherently enforce the practitioner’s self-application or congruence.

Additionally, Grinder started to question other aspects of how Classic Code NLP approached change. One issue was an “excessive focus on the client’s conscious efforts to find solutions”

in many classic techniques (Welcome to the home of New Code NLP). The Meta Model exemplifies a very conscious, analytical approach – the practitioner asks logical questions, the client considers answers. While effective, it largely engages the conscious mind.

Grinder and his new collaborators began to suspect that deeper, more ecological change might require engaging the unconscious mind more directly. The great hypnotherapist Milton Erickson, whom Bandler and Grinder also modeled, had demonstrated how powerful unconscious communication could be. By the mid-1980s, Grinder was looking for ways to incorporate more of that leverage into NLP change formats, reducing reliance on overthinking or overtalking a problem.

It’s important to note that during this period (late ’70s to mid ’80s), Grinder’s own intellectual horizons widened. He collaborated with linguist and anthropologist Judith DeLozier, co- authoring Turtles All the Way Down: Prerequisites to Personal Genius (published 1987, based on seminars in 1983–84). Turtles All the Way Down is a much more philosophical and exploratory work than the early NLP books. It delves into concepts from Gregory Bateson’s cybernetics and Carlos Castaneda’s teachings, with a strong emphasis on epistemology (how we know what we know), unconscious learning, and the integration of multiple perspectives. In this work, Grinder and DeLozier hardly mention the Meta Model by name, but the principles behind it continue to play a role, and we see Grinder relating those linguistic insights to broader concepts.

For example, Grinder recounts a conversation with Gregory Bateson, who was an influential mentor to the early NLP developers. Bateson had been working on the idea that the fundamental unit of mind is “a difference that makes a difference”, and that to perceive a difference, you actually need two descriptions (double description) of something. In Turtles, Judith DeLozier notes:

“John and I and Richard were talking with Gregory Bateson and he looked at us and said, ‘This notion of nominalization is brilliant.’” Bateson appreciated the Meta Model concept of nominalization because he had spent time arguing that nothing exists in isolation – meaning is generated by contrast or difference between at least two elements. The term “difference,” as Bateson used it, is actually a nominalization (it’s a noun that implies a relation). Grinder points out to the seminar group that anyone “trained in the Meta-Model” would naturally realize that ‘difference’ is a nominalization which comes from a two-place predicate… from the linguistic structure itself you know that there were at least two relata (two things in relationship) that this thing called ‘difference’ was the relationship between.’ ([John Grinder, Judith DeLozier - Turtles All the Way Down Prereqsites to Personal Genius-Grinder, DeLozier & Associates. (1995).pdf] (file://file- YcErURPJ3f7wPzbeHMrML2#:~:text=Bateson%20and%20he%20looked%20at,description%20be cause%20the%20word%20%27difference)) . In simpler terms, the Meta Model insight (recognizing nominalizations) gave a quick, practical confirmation to Bateson’s abstract point: whenever someone says “a difference,” the very grammar (a noun with no specified subject/object)

presupposes that two things are being implicitly compared. This is a beautiful example of how the linguistic sharpening of the Meta Model intersected with high-level epistemology. Bateson’s praise – “This notion of nominalization is brilliant” – validated Grinder’s linguistic approach as not just therapy gimmickry but something touching the fundamental patterns of thought .

Thus, during the transitional period, Grinder increasingly connected the Meta Model to deeper frameworks. The Meta Model became, in his view, one instantiation of a larger principle:

modeling and making explicit the hidden patterns in our experience. Language was one domain for this; by the mid-80s, he was exploring others (physical movement, “know-nothing states,”

multiple perspectives, etc.). Yet, he never abandoned the importance of language. In the preface of Turtles All the Way Down, he lists “Meta-models of language” alongside representational systems and other tools as “cornerstones in the exploration of [the] mapping between sense impressions and concepts.” . The Meta Model was still fundamental to mapping experience; it was simply now joined by additional “maps for the same territory.”

One important refinement Grinder introduced in this era was the idea of “second attention” or the “know-nothing state,” concepts which emphasize the role of the unconscious mind and the value of suspending one’s internal chatter. In Turtles, on the very first day of the seminar, Grinder asks participants for “full attention” – both the usual conscious attention and the “second, less accessible part of us” that contains a different kind of wisdom . This second attention can be equated to what NLP calls the unconscious mind. Grinder cultivated exercises in Turtles to help people enter a “know-nothing state,” essentially a state of suspended presuppositions, where one doesn’t jump to categorize or interpret experience. While not explicitly about language, this concept complements the Meta Model: to truly listen and respond flexibly (as the Meta Model demands), it helps to quiet one’s internal filters and assumptions (a know-nothing state).

We see here a shift in emphasis from technique to state. The classic Meta Model is very technique-oriented (specific questions to ask in response to specific language patterns). Grinder’s evolving view by the 1980s was that the state of the practitioner is crucial for the effective use of any technique. If the practitioner is in a open, attentive, “know-nothing” state – fully present with the client and not contaminating their perceptions with personal biases – they will calibrate better and apply the Meta Model (or any model) far more gracefully. This idea foreshadows Grinder’s later insistence in New Code NLP that “state precedes content” and that an NLP practitioner must apply the patterns to themselves and achieve personal congruence.

Another development in this transitional period was integrating the Meta Model with non- linguistic patterns. For instance, The Structure of Magic II discussed incongruence (when a person’s words, tone, and body language don’t align) and offered “meta tactics” for dealing with it. Grinder and Bandler described how one might match or mismatch a client’s representational system predicates when asking Meta Model questions . For example, if a client is primarily using visual language, phrasing a question visually (“What do you see that makes you believe X?”) might yield better results than a mismatched question (“How do you feel X?”). Conversely, deliberate switching of representational frames (a “meta-tactic”) could be used to break a stuck state .

They also noted that the Meta Model can be thought of as part of a larger communication cycle with the Milton Model (the inverse language patterns used in hypnosis). In Turtles, Grinder asks participants to think about “where Meta-Model/Milton-Model work fits” into the bigger picture of communication and change – indicating that by the mid-80s he saw these as complementary tools that needed to be orchestrated within a broader strategy.

In summary, the late 1970s and early 1980s saw John Grinder reflect on and extend the Meta Model rather than fundamentally change it. He validated its power (as in the Bateson anecdote)

and continued teaching it, but also became aware of how it could be misapplied (overly conscious, not self-applied, used without proper state). We can think of this period as “transitional refinement”: Grinder was gathering insight into how the Meta Model could be taught or used better, setting the stage for a more radical shift that would come with the New Code. These considerations directly informed Grinder’s next big project: the creation of New Code NLP in the mid-1980s (formalized in the 1990s). Alongside his partner Carmen Bostic St. Clair, Grinder sought to “re-code” NLP, preserving its genius but remedying its weak spots.
