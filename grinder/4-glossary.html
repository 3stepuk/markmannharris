4. Glossary of Key Terms and Distinctions (Grinder’s NLP Lexicon)

To assist in understanding Grinder’s terminology across his works, below is a glossary of key terms and linguistic distinctions, with definitions as used in context and references to source explanations:

Deep Structure refers to the full underlying meaning (conceptual structure) of a sentence – essentially what one intends to express. Surface Structure is the actual sentence as spoken or written. In the process of speaking, certain transformations occur that map the deep structure to the surface structure, often deleting or abstracting information. Grinder’s Meta Model focuses on recovering deep structure from surface structure. For example, if the deep thought is “I am sad because I lost my job,” the person might utter a surface sentence like “I’m just a failure” – which has deleted the cause and generalized the feeling. The Meta Model question would help retrieve the full deep meaning (“What happened that makes you feel that way?”). Magic I notes: “The Surface Structure… is a representation of the full linguistic representation… the Deep Structure. In the case wherein deletion has occurred, the Surface Structure is missing [something]…” .

information out. Linguistically, it refers to sentences where something essential is missing or unspecified. Grinder’s definition: “Deletion: One of the three universals of human modeling; the process by which selected portions of the world are excluded from the representation… Within language systems, deletion is a transformational process…” . In everyday terms, deletion shows up when someone speaks vaguely or omits key details (“I’m upset” – upset about what? Or “This is too hard” – what specifically is hard?). The Meta Model challenge for deletion is to ask questions that retrieve the omitted information (“Upset about what, specifically?”). Deletions can be simple deletion (missing context or detail), unspecified nouns/pronouns (unclear who or what is being referred to: e.g. “They don’t understand” – who is ‘they’?), or unspecified verbs (unclear action: e.g.

“She hurt me” – how exactly?). By recovering deletions, the client’s communication becomes more complete and actionable. (Sources: The Structure of Magic I, Chapter 4; Whispering, Ch. 8)

a specific experience and drawing a broad rule or conclusion from it, sometimes bigger than justified. Grinder’s definition: “Generalization: the process by which a specific experience comes to represent the entire category of which it is a member.” . For example, one failure leading to “I’ll never succeed at anything” is a generalization. Generalization is useful for learning (we form concepts and expectations via generalization) but can limit us when overextended. In language, generalizations show up as universal quantifiers (“all, every, never, always, none”) or sweeping statements.

The Meta Model challenges generalizations by seeking exceptions or specificity: if someone says “Nobody cares about me,” the practitioner might ask “Nobody? Really no one at all?” to prompt the client to find even a single counterexample, thereby loosening the absolute belief (The Distinctive Features of New Code NLP - NLP Academy). Another kind of generalization in language is modal operators (see below), which generalize across contexts rules about necessity or possibility (e.g. “I can’t X,” “I must Y” – implying in all relevant situations this holds). (Sources: Magic I, pp.14–17; Whispering, discussion on linguistic ontology)

Distortion is altering experience by adding or imbuing meaning that isn’t strictly given by sensory data. Grinder in 1975: “Distortion is the process which allows us to make shifts in our experience of sensory data… [It’s] the process whereby the relationships among the parts of a model are represented differently from the relationships they are supposed to represent.” . Distortion covers a variety of phenomena: Mind Reading (assuming you know someone’s internal state without direct communication), Cause-Effect (believing X directly causes Y in a way that eliminates choice), Complex Equivalence (equating two things as the same, e.g. “He didn’t smile at me = He hates me”), Nominalization (turning a process into a thing, e.g. “a failure”), etc. These are all distortions because the person’s map is not a straightforward reflection of reality; they have added interpretations or turned dynamic processes into static conclusions. The Meta Model challenges distortions by questioning the basis:

“How do you know?” for mind reading , “How specifically does X cause Y?” for cause-effect , “What do you mean by that?” or “According to whom?” for complex equivalences and lost performatives, and turning nominalizations back into verbs (e.g.

“What is the process of X really?”). The aim is to reconnect the client with sensory evidence and personal agency. (Source: Magic I, pp.59–81; Turtles, p.68 where Bateson praises nominalization concept )

internal state (thoughts, feelings, intentions) without direct evidence. E.g. “My boss hates me”

(without the boss saying so), or “You know what I mean” (assuming the listener does know).

Grinder: “This class… involves the belief on the part of the speaker that one person can know what another person is thinking or feeling without a direct communication.” . Mind reading is challenged by asking “How do you know? Did they tell you?” or asking for the source of that information. Often, it reveals the person is guessing or projecting. (Source: Magic I, p.104 )

The Meta Model response is to challenge the inevitability: e.g. “How exactly does my arriving late cause you to feel unloved?” or “Have you ever seen someone not get angry in that situation?” This helps the client realize there might be intermediate steps or interpretations involved. The Meta Model challenge is to break that equivalence by asking “How does not calling mean not caring?” or suggest alternative meanings (“Could it mean something else?”). E.g. “There is no communication in this team” (communication nominalized), or “I have a lot of anxiety” (anxiety here is a nominalized form of the process “to be anxious” or “to anxiously anticipate”). Nominalizations are identified by the Meta Model by asking if the noun is an intangible concept and if you can’t put it in a wheelbarrow (a classic NLP test: you can put a “book” in a wheelbarrow but not “justice” or “sadness” – those are nominalizations).

Grinder in Magic I says “The purpose of recognizing nominalizations is to assist the client in re- connecting his model with the ongoing dynamic processes of life. Specifically, reversing nominalizations assists the client in coming to see that what he had considered an event is actually a process that can be changed.” ([OceanofPDF.com The Structure of Magic I - Richard Bandler.pdf] (file:// file-2gmdNaGgvKsHeLvhjcvgfF#:~:text=The%20linguistic%20process%20of%20nominalization,o ccurs%20in%20natural%20language%20systems)) . To challenge a nominalization, one can ask the noun as a verb: “Who is not communicating what to whom?” (turn “communication” back into “communicating”), or “How are you anxiously anticipating things?” instead of “anxiety.” This brings motion and possibility for change back into the client’s language. (Source: Magic I, pp.74– 79 ; Turtles, p.68 where nominalization’s brilliance is noted )

Necessity (must, have to, should/shouldn’t, ought, need to) and Modal Operators of Possibility (can/can’t, may, possible/impossible, allowed to, etc.). These indicate generalizations about what is necessary or possible. For example, “I can’t ask for a raise,” or “I must always be polite.” They often hint at internal rules or imposed limits. The Meta Model treatment is to challenge the rule: For necessity – “What would happen if you didn’t?” (e.g. “What would happen if you weren’t always polite?” to uncover the fear or belief), and for possibility – “What prevents you?” or “What would happen if you could?” (“What makes you think you can’t ask for a raise?”).

The idea is to loosen the constraint by examining its basis or exception. Grinder (with DeLozier) in Patterns II and others elaborated these as special cases of generalization. (Sources: Magic I doesn’t explicitly label “modal operators” but addresses these under generalizations; later elaborated in NLP literature and implied in Whispering ([NLP -Whispering in the Wind - John Grinder.pdf] (file://file- HoDGNjkKzTDA3WNZFFUnLa#:~:text=In%20the%20first%20of%20the,below%20demonstrates %20the%20point%2C%20Client)).)

omitted. It’s essentially a rule or belief stated as fact. For example, “It’s bad to show weakness,” or “Cheating is wrong,” or “You don’t do that.” The speaker doesn’t say who thinks so – the source of the belief is left out (deleted). It’s “lost” because the performer of the evaluation is missing. The Meta Model challenge is “According to whom?” or “Who says so?” or “How do you know that [criterion]?” This prompts the person to either identify the source (maybe “my mother always said that”) or realize it’s just an opinion, not a universal truth. (Source: Magic I implies this pattern in discussing unspecified sources of judgments; term “lost performative” introduced in later NLP usage like Frogs into Princes).

Well-formedness conditions in therapy include: having no deletions that cloud meaning, no nominalizations (processes should be seen as processes), clear referential indices (know who is involved), etc. “Well-formed in therapy is a set of conditions which the Surface Structures the client uses… must meet in order to be acceptable [for change].” If a client’s statement doesn’t meet these, the therapist uses the Meta Model to gather more info until it is well-formed. For instance, a desired outcome “I want better communication” is not well-formed (nominalization, unspecified with whom). Through Meta Model questions this can be turned into a well-formed outcome like “I want to spend 15 minutes each evening talking openly with my spouse about our days” – which is specific and testable.

Grinder saw these conditions as “the appropriate grammar for therapy” – a way to ensure the change work has solid, specific material to work with. (Sources: Magic II, pp.4–6 )

Grinder always stressed calibration as the fundamental NLP skill – the ability to notice what response you’re getting and adjust accordingly. In New Code, calibration to unconscious signals (like involuntary muscle movements signaling yes/no) is crucial. Calibration isn’t a Meta Model term per se, but it underlies effective Meta Model use (knowing from subtle reactions if a question hit a nerve or if the person is accessing something). By the time of Whispering, Grinder tied calibration to the idea of “zeroing out” conscious bias and truly paying attention. (Source: Whispering, where he calls calibration “the most fundamental of all NLP skills” and builds new code on it ).

It’s analogous to beginner’s mind or being fully present in the now. In a know-nothing state, a practitioner doesn’t pre-judge what a client’s problem is or what technique to use; they attend carefully and let their unconscious and calibration guide them. Grinder introduced this as part of New Code NLP, drawing from his experience in martial arts and sports where being in “the zone” requires not anticipating consciously .

He notes that he and Bandler inadvertently used a know-nothing state when they did their original modeling work (they suspended what they “knew” and just observed). In practice, one might achieve a know-nothing state by brief centering exercises, then interacting with the client with genuine curiosity via something like the Verbal Package. (Sources: Turtles All the Way Down, 1987; Whispering, 2001 )

work co-created by Grinder and Bandler (roughly 1974–1980s) – this includes the Meta Model, Milton Model, anchoring, submodalities, reframing, etc. and the style in which those were applied, often content-specific and practitioner-driven. New Code NLP (developed by Grinder & Bostic from mid-80s to present) is a reformation of NLP that emphasizes working at the level of states, unconscious choice, and simplicity of formats. Key differences: New Code engages the unconscious in every step, uses content-free games (like alphabet game, dancing with eyes closed, etc.) to create high performance states, insists on the practitioner’s self-application and congruence, and simplifies verbal interventions (the Verbal Package) to focus on what’s essential (like outcomes, intentions, state). As Grinder succinctly put it, “Classic Code… contained coding flaws” that New Code corrects (Welcome to the home of New Code NLP) – these flaws being things like conscious over-involvement and lack of self-application. The Meta Model (Classic Code) vs. Verbal Package (New Code) is one microcosm of these differences. New Code patterns are fewer but broadly applicable, often utilizing unconscious processes and games for change. (Source: Whispering in the Wind, 2001, which extensively contrasts the two ([The Distinctive Features of New Code NLP - NLP Academy] (https://www.nlpacademy.co.uk/blog/view/the_new_code_nlp_difference/ #:~:text=John%20Grinder%20said%20the%20following,number%20of%20serious%20coding%20 flaws)) (The Distinctive Features of New Code NLP - NLP Academy))

minimal set of language patterns (questions/frames) used to ensure clarity and precision in communication, focusing mainly on challenging deletions and vague language while framing intention and maintaining rapport. It includes “reduced questions, explicit framing and refined verbal distinctions (description, interpretation, evaluation)” . Unlike the full Meta Model, the Verbal Package doesn’t overtly name every distortion; it simply equips the practitioner to get the needed info in a gentle way. Think of it as “Meta Model 2.0”: leaner, friendlier, and used with conscious awareness of context and state. If the Meta Model was a toolbox of 12 wrenches, the Verbal Package is like a versatile wrench that adjusts to fit most bolts, plus a guide on how to use it without overtightening. Grinder listed the Verbal Package as one of the core patterns of New Code NLP .

statements that Grinder emphasizes in later teaching (drawn from earlier communication models, likely Gregory Bateson’s influence and general semantics). A description is a report of sensory- observable facts (who, what, when, where behaviorally). An interpretation is an inference or meaning read into those facts. An evaluation is a value judgment or emotional appraisal of those facts. For example, if a client says “My husband doesn’t talk to me as much these days. I think he’s losing interest in me. It’s horrible,” the first sentence is largely a description (with maybe a slight deletion of context), the second sentence is an interpretation (her meaning: husband losing interest), and the third is an evaluation (“horrible”). In the Verbal Package, a practitioner will recognize these layers and might respond by first clarifying the description (e.g. how specifically frequency has changed), then gently questioning the interpretation (“What makes you think he’s losing interest? Could there be other reasons?”), and acknowledging the evaluation (“It feels horrible to you”). By separating these, the practitioner helps the client see what’s fact versus meaning versus feeling. Grinder suggests using these distinctions as a conscious tool to keep conversations clear. This helps avoid miscommunication (people often argue over interpretations or evaluations while assuming the descriptions).

These terms form a vocabulary that spans Grinder’s early and later work. Early on, deletion, distortion, generalization, etc., were introduced; later, terms like know-nothing state and Verbal Package entered. Understanding them as defined above provides a solid grasp of how Grinder views communication and change. They interplay: for instance, in a know-nothing state, a practitioner listens for deletions and uses the Verbal Package to ask for specifics, distinguishing description from interpretation, all while ensuring the interaction is well-framed and well-formed in a therapeutic sense.
