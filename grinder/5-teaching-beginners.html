These questions should be asked one at a time, and allow the person to answer fully. Avoid stacking multiple questions or interrogating. A calm, patient, one-question-at-a-time approach works best. The client might answer one, then stop; you listen and see if any vagueness remains, then ask another. For instance:

In that snippet, the doula used who specifically and what specifically (did they do), and paraphrased at the end to confirm understanding.

3. Paraphrase and Reflect Back. After you gather specific information, summarize it back in your own words to ensure you got it right and to let the client feel heard. Paraphrase both content and, when appropriate, emotion/intention. For example:

tried to go over your birth plan, he kind of brushed it off, which left you feeling ignored. Is that accurate?”

thought my ideas were silly.” (which is even more info – maybe an interpretation or an evaluation).

Paraphrasing acts as a reality check for both of you. It often prompts the client to clarify further or to feel relief that someone finally understands. In doing so, it naturally moves their experience from an emotional swirl to a more organized narrative (a mild form of therapeutic effect in itself). Grinder added paraphrase to the Verbal Package to ensure the practitioner is “gathering correct information” (The Distinctive Features of New Code NLP - NLP Academy) – it’s a safeguard against misunderstanding.

For beginners, a good habit is: after every significant client statement, especially after you’ve asked a couple of specifying questions, summarize: “So, [X] happened, and you’re feeling [Y] because [Z]. Did I get that right?” This also allows the client to correct you if needed. In birth contexts, emotions run high; paraphrasing shows empathy. E.g., “You’ve been feeling pretty anxious ever since that difficult birth class, because it brought up memories of your sister’s complications, right?” If correct, the client often visibly relaxes – someone finally put their unsaid connections into words.

Always maintain that empathic stance. If the client seems threatened or defensive, back off and return to validating and clarifying. The Whispering text suggests that the Verbal Package uses “relevancy challenges” and refined distinctions instead of blunt confrontation .

In practice, a relevancy challenge would be: if a client has a strong belief like “I can’t do this,” you might respond, “I know you feel that way now. If that thought comes during labor, is it okay with you if I remind you of how well you did in practice? Or would you prefer I just hold your hand quietly? You’re respecting the belief yet planting a seed that it might not be 100% true always.

For a beginner, the key steps illustrated are:

1. Hear the client out. Encourage them to talk about their concern freely first.

2. Ask specific questions to clarify any and all vagueness.

3. Listen and empathize – show you understand the factual content and emotional content by summarizing and validating.

4. Focus on what they want (e.g. safe birth, being conscious, etc. – positive outcome).

5. Address distortions delicately – in this case, the client recognized differences in her mom’s case versus hers, which already helps. If she hadn’t, the doula might have pointed it out gently (“Your blood pressure has been fine, which is a good sign; your situation is different from your mom’s, though I know that doesn’t erase the worry.”).

6. Formulate action or reframe: come up with a constructive step (talk to OB, make plan)

which gives the client a sense of control – an antidote to feeling dread.

7. Check in on how the client feels after that discussion. If still very fearful, might need to do more (perhaps a relaxation exercise or bring in the OB sooner).

8. Keep rapport and encouragement: end on a note that underscores the client’s capability or support system.

For teaching a class of birth professionals this method, one might do role-plays: have one person be a client with a vague fear or complaint, and the other practice using these Verbal Package techniques to draw out specifics and provide support. Afterward, the group can discuss what worked – usually, they’ll find the client role-player feels much better when the practitioner asks specific questions and listens than if the practitioner just said “Don’t worry, it’ll be fine” or offered stock reassurances. In summary, the Verbal Package empowers professionals to be truly client-centered in communication. By mastering it, a birth professional can transform a panicked “I’m terrified something will go wrong!” into a calm conversation about specific concerns, proactive planning, and emotional support. John Grinder’s journey from the comprehensive Meta Model to the elegant Verbal Package means that we don’t need a linguistics degree to apply these insights – we just need practice in listening well and asking the right questions. As Grinder and Bostic wrote, their thinking moved toward a “minimal model… functionally equivalent to the full meta model” . For us, that minimal model boils down to: Be curious, be clear, be respectful – and the rest will follow. Finally, as a future world expert or master practitioner in NLP and communication, keep an attitude of lifelong learning. Grinder’s own evolution teaches us that models can be refined continually. Use the Verbal Package as a foundation, and as you gain experience, you will develop your own artistry in communication, just as Grinder did – always aiming for that magical combination of clarity, depth, and human connection that truly helps people.
