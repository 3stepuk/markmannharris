The 12 Master Keys

The Explorer's ultimate cheat sheet.

Most people think talking about problems is just “sharing a story.” But Master Explorers know a secret: words are code. When your friend talks about their feelings, their brain is running a live, invisible video game engine.

You don't need a million different questions to explore this map. You just need twelve keys. These twelve exact questions are designed to bypass your friend's normal, arguing brain and speak directly to their inner World Engine.

Before you use them on anything difficult, use two of them on a place that holds. Key 2, the Magnifying Glass, and Key 10, the Quest Compass, on a bench or a nan's kitchen will teach you more about these keys than a whole afternoon of reading. That is Module 0, and it takes ten minutes.

Group 1 — Building the map

Form and space. These keys turn blurry, confusing feelings into high-definition objects you can actually look at and work with.

Key 1 · The Flashlight

Peripheral detail.

“And is there anything else about [X]?”

It shines a light on the edges of a landmark. Instead of moving to a new part of the map, it makes the brain look closer at what is already there — and usually reveals hidden safe zones.

Key 2 · The Magnifying Glass

High-definition rendering.

“And what kind of [X] is that [X]?”

It forces the graphics engine to upgrade a blurry idea — “I'm scared” — into a specific physical object: “It's an icy block.” Once it's an object, the brain knows how to deal with it.

Key 3 · The Map Pin

Absolute coordinates.

“And where is [X]?”

Feelings are scary when they are everywhere at once. A Map Pin forces the brain to give the feeling exact coordinates — “It's in my throat” — and that calms the nervous system, because the monster is now trapped in one spot.

Key 4 · The Radar Scan

Refined coordinates.

“And whereabouts?”

A softer version of the Map Pin. If the feeling is “in my chest”, “whereabouts?” invites the brain to scan the area and find the exact pixel where the glitch lives.

Key 5 · The Shape-Shifter

Turning data into metaphors.

“And that's [X] like what?”

A magic trick. If your friend has a physical feeling — “my stomach hurts” — this asks the brain to turn it into a symbolic object: “It's like a washing machine spinning.” Working with the washing machine is much easier than trying to fix a stomach ache.

Group 2 — Time travel

Sequence and backstory. These keys track the timeline of the glitch without ever asking “why?” — because “why” just makes the brain invent excuses. We want to see the actual code.

Key 6 · The Play Button

Moving one frame.

“And then what happens?”

Moves the game forward exactly one step. It shows you the immediate next thing the glitch does.

Key 7 · The Fast-Forward Button

The domino effect.

“And what happens next?”

Similar to the Play Button, but it lets the game run a little longer, to see the bigger consequence of the glitch.

Key 8 · The Rewind Button

Finding the trigger.

“And what happens just before [X]?”

The ultimate glitch-tracker. It walks the code backward to the exact millisecond before the trap snapped shut — so the brain can see the trigger before the panic started.

Key 9 · The Origin Tracker

Deep backstory.

“And where could [X] come from?”

Probes deep into the game's lore. It looks past the immediate trigger to the original source of the landmark — “that heavy rock came from a deep, dark cave underground.”

Group 3 — Changing the level

Intent and upgrades. These keys don't just look at the map — they test it, challenge it, and help the World Engine write new code to beat the level.

Key 10 · The Quest Compass

The main objective.

“And what would you like to have happen?”

Instantly stops the brain staring at the problem and makes it look toward a solution. It sets the new main mission.

Pro tip — ask the glitch: you can also ask, “And what would that heavy rock like to have happen?” Treat the glitch like a character. Sometimes the rock just wants to be a pebble.

Key 11 · The Blueprint

System requirements.

“And what needs to happen for [X] to [Y]?”

Once a new objective is set, this asks the engine to print the exact blueprint to get there. It maps the trail between the glitch and the upgrade.

Key 12 · The System Check

Testing the code.

“And can [X] [Y]?” — e.g. “And can that rock turn into a pebble?”

Tests whether the game has enough power to do the upgrade. If the body relaxes and they say yes, the code works. If they hold their breath and say no, you need the Flashlight to find more safe zones first.

Group 4 — How to speak the code

Delivery modes. How you ask matters as much as what you ask.

Mode 1 · The Deep Scan

Full syntax.

When your friend is really stuck or upset, use the full, repetitive code to keep their map steady.

Repeat their exact words + “And when…” + ask the key. — “And you feel a knot… and when you feel a knot… whereabouts is that knot?”

It slows everything down, makes them feel completely heard, and pins the brain to the exact spot.

Mode 2 · The Co-Op Chat

Casual code.

If you are just hanging out in the cafeteria, the Deep Scan sounds like a robot. Casual mode is fine — as long as you still obey the No Littering rule.

A quick bridge + ask the key. — “So there's a knot… whereabouts is it?”

It sounds like a normal conversation, but it still uses the keys to help their brain map the Secret World.

Ready, player one

You don't need to memorise all twelve keys right away. If you only ever use the Magnifying Glass — “what kind of [X]?” — and the Quest Compass — “what would you like to happen?” — you will already be a better guide than 99% of adults.

Keep this cheat sheet in your back pocket. The next time a friend's game crashes, you'll know exactly which buttons to press to help them reboot.
