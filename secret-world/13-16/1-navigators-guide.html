Module 1

The Navigator's Guide

How to help someone explore their own world without changing their map.

Everyone has a world

When someone says “I've got a heavy knot in my stomach about the maths test”, or “I'm boiling with anger”, they are not only using words. In their world there is something hot and tight and real.

The instinct is to step in and fix it — to say “just untie it” or “turn the stove off”. It doesn't work, and it usually leaves them feeling less understood than before.

You can't move things in someone else's world. What you can do is ask questions that help them see it clearly for themselves. Once they can see it, they usually know what to do next.

That is the whole method. Everything else in this guide is detail.

1

The three rules

Don't litter

Leave no trace of your own stuff. If they say “I'm stuck in a dark cave”, you don't ask “are there bats in there?” — because now you have put bats in their cave. You use their exact words, and only their exact words.

In the literature, this is Non-Contamination.

Change the camera angle

When their world gets too much, they are standing too close to it. If they say “the monster is right in my face”, the problem is not the monster; it is the distance. Asking where they are standing while they look at it moves them back a step. From there it can be looked at.

In the literature, this is Epistemological Distance.

The map is live

Their world renders as you look at it. Ask about a heavy rock and the rock may turn into a balloon, or melt into sand. Your questions make the world move. That is not a fault in the method; it is the method working.

In the literature, this is the Psychoactive Landscape.

2

What you will find in there

Landmarks

A specific thing in their world — a feeling (“a tight chest”), an object (“a brick wall”), a state (“a dark cloud”). Landmarks are what a map is made of.

In the literature, these are Nodes.

The thread

The invisible line that shows where something came from and where it is going next. Follow it, and the map starts to make sense.

In the literature, these are Vectors.

The Glitch

A loop someone keeps walking round. “I get stressed, so I stop doing my homework, which makes me more stressed, so I keep not doing it.” Nothing is broken; the system is repeating.

In the literature, this is a Problem Loop.

The Safe Zone

A place on their map that holds. Even when everything else is a storm, there is somewhere quiet and solid — “a steady feeling in my feet”. Your job is to help them find it, not to build it. Module 0 is the whole of this.

In the literature, this is a Resource Space.

Body Clues

When the map changes, the body says so. A long breath out. Shoulders dropping. Eyes going up and away. A door has just unlocked.

In the literature, these are Somatic Endpoints.

The Glitch Trap

Two options, both bad. “If I tell the teacher, my friends will be angry. If I don't, I'm in trouble.” They are stuck, and advice makes them defend the trap. You map both sides, and the space between them, until they spot the way out.

In the literature, this is a Double Bind.

3

The tools

Six questions. Ask them slowly, in their words, and wait for them to look around and answer.

The Magnifying Glass

“And what kind of [X] is that [X]?”

They say they have a headache. You ask what kind of headache that headache is. They have to look closer. “A spiky, red one.”

The Map Pin

“And where is [X]?” — or — “And does [X] have a size or a shape?”

It pins the thing to a place. “Where is that spiky red headache?” — “Behind my left eye.”

The Flashlight

“And is there anything else about [X]?”

It lights the edges of the landmark, where the detail you haven't heard yet is hiding.

The Rewind

“And what happens just before [X]?”

It follows the thread backwards, to where the loop started.

The Fast-Forward

“And then what happens?”

It follows the thread forwards, to what the loop causes next.

The Compass

“And when [X] is there, what would you like to have happen?”

The strongest of the six. It turns them from the problem towards a direction, and the system starts working on that instead.

4

A real conversation

Two friends, the night before a presentation. Watch how the Navigator never gives advice — and how the friend works it out anyway.

Friend

“I'm so worried about the presentation tomorrow. I feel completely frozen.”A Landmark appears: frozen.

Navigator

“And you feel completely frozen. And when you feel frozen, what kind of frozen is that frozen?”the Magnifying Glass

Friend

“It's like I'm trapped in a block of ice.”

Navigator

“And you're trapped in a block of ice. And where is that block of ice?”the Map Pin

Friend

“It's wrapped all around my legs and my stomach. It makes me feel stuck.”

Navigator

“Wrapped all around your legs and your stomach. And when you're trapped in that block of ice… what would you like to have happen?”the Compass

Friend

“I just want it to melt so I can walk to the front of the room normally.”

Navigator

“And you want it to melt so you can walk to the front. And is there anything else about melting?”the Flashlight

Friend

“Well… my hands are actually pretty warm right now. Like there's a lot of heat in my palms.”A Safe Zone appears.

Navigator

“And there's a lot of heat in your palms. And when there's heat in your palms, what happens to that block of ice?”connecting the Safe Zone to the Landmark

Friend

“The heat kind of pushes down. The ice is cracking a bit. It's not so tight any more.”A long breath. A Body Clue.

The Navigator never said “just relax”, and never said “imagine them in their underwear”. They mapped the ice and the heat, and the friend's own system did the rest.
