Module 2

The Expansion Pack

When the map is confusing: how things connect, what to do with a trap, and why the room matters.

When one landmark isn't the whole story

If you've mapped a Safe Zone and you can use the Magnifying Glass and the Map Pin, you have the basics. You also know the rule that matters most: don't rearrange someone else's map.

But a map is rarely one thing. Two parts of it are usually affecting each other, and sometimes someone is caught between two bad options with no obvious way out.

This module is four things: the connections, the trap, the room, and the pause.

1

The invisible strings

Step on a red button in a game and a door opens across the room. Nothing visible joins them, but they are wired together. A map works the same way: one landmark usually affects another.

If someone has a heavy rock in their stomach and a dark cloud over their head, those two are not sitting there separately. They are interacting. Your job is to find the wire.

The wiring questions

“And is there a connection between [A] and [B]?”

“And when [A] is doing that, what happens to [B]?”

Ask about the rock and the cloud, and they may see it: “Oh — the cloud is raining on the rock and making it heavier.” Once they can see the wiring, they can start to rewire it.

In the literature, these are Systemic Relationships and Vectors.

2

The room is part of the tool

Sometimes someone is stuck looking at a problem from the same chair. In the map, it is as if the camera is pinned in a corner.

Physical movement changes that. Move to a different spot in the room and the brain opens different files.

How to use it

1. Stop asking about the map for a moment.

2. Ask. “And if you could move to any other spot in this room to think about this… where would you go?”

3. Let them move. The floor, the window, the doorway. Anywhere they choose.

4. Ask again. “And from here… what do you know now about [the problem]?”

It sounds like a trick. It isn't. A new physical position is a new angle, and a new angle is often the whole difference.

In the literature, this is the Clean Space Protocol.

3

The Glitch Trap

The hardest thing on the map: being caught between two bad options.

“If I try out, I might fail and look stupid.”
“If I don't try out, I'll be angry at myself forever.”

They are frozen. Most people give advice here — “just try out, who cares what people think” — and the advice makes them defend the trap. Don't.

Map both sides, then map the space between them.

How to map a trap

1. The first bad thing. “And what kind of ‘look stupid’ is that?”

2. The second bad thing. “And where is that ‘angry at myself forever’?”

3. The question in the middle. “And what is between [the first] and [the second]?”

Looking at the space between two traps is where people find the path they couldn't see. It is usually not a compromise. It is somewhere else entirely.

In the literature, this is a Double Bind.

4

Let it settle

You ask a good question and something shifts. The block of ice melts into a warm puddle, and they take a breath.

Don't say “great, sorted”. Don't move on. When a map changes, it takes a moment to save.

Slow down and stay with it:

“And now it's a warm puddle. And when it's a warm puddle… what happens to the rest of the map?”

“And is there anything else about this warm puddle?”

“And as you look at this warm puddle, what do you know now?”

Give it a few minutes. The change is still landing. It isn't finished until they look settled and say they are.

In the literature, this is Landscape Maturing.

5

A conversation with all four in it

Friend

“I feel completely torn in half about this argument with Sam. I want to apologise, but I also want to protect myself, because they were mean.”A Glitch Trap.

Navigator

“And you feel torn in half. And when you are torn in half, what kind of torn in half is that?”

Friend

“It's like two ropes pulling my chest in opposite directions.”

Navigator

“And two ropes. And is there a connection between the rope that wants to apologise and the rope that wants to protect you?”the invisible strings

Friend

“Yeah… they're both tied to the same knot in the middle of my ribs.”

Navigator

“And they're tied to a knot. And if you could move to any spot in this room to look at that knot, where would you go?”the room

Friend

“I want to stand up and look out the window.”Walks to the window.

Navigator

“And from there, looking out the window… what do you know now about that knot?”

Friend

“From here… I can see the knot isn't actually that tight. If I drop my hands, the ropes kind of fall away. I don't have to pull on either of them right now. I can wait.”

Navigator

“And you can wait. And when you drop your hands and the ropes fall away… what happens to your chest?”letting it settle

Friend

“It feels wider. It feels peaceful.”

Nothing was solved by the Navigator. The room changed the angle, the strings showed the structure, and the friend found the answer.
