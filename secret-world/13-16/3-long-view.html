Module 3

The Long View

The engine, the door back to the real world, and what to do when the map is yours.

Where you are now

You can find the connections, map a trap, and use a room to change the angle. That is most of the skill.

What is left is the part almost nobody gets right. It is the difference between being useful and being in the way.

1

Trust the engine

Think of a game where the world builds itself as you walk. Cut down a tree and the game works out where it falls and what the animals do about it. Something is running in the background, keeping it coherent.

People have that too. Their system wants to solve the problem. It is not waiting for you to supply the answer.

When a dark cloud turns into a golden shield, it wasn't because you said so. Their system decided the cloud wasn't working any more and upgraded it.

So don't steer it. If you think the shield should be a sword, keep that to yourself. Try to write their map for them and it stops working. Ask your questions and let the engine do the writing.

In the literature, this is Unconscious Self-Organisation.

2

The door back

The session went well. The knot in their stomach untied itself and became something calm. They feel fine.

Tomorrow they have to walk into the same school, sit the same test, see the same person. How does the calm thing come with them?

You build a door. Ask a question that looks forward, while the map is calm and they are relaxed:

“And as you take this with you… what will you notice when you go in tomorrow?”

“And what is the very first thing you will do differently when [the test] happens, now that you have this [calm thing]?”

They are not predicting the future. They are placing the resource in it. When they walk in the next day, it is already there.

In the literature, this is Future Pacing, or the Ecological Bridge.

3

Your own map

Everything so far has been about someone else. Your own map is harder, because you can't easily ask yourself a question out loud without the thinking getting in the way.

So get it out of your head. Paper and a pencil are enough.

How

1. Start with a place that holds. A bench, a shed, a song. Ask: “What kind of place is that place?” If you'd rather start with a problem, that works too; the steps are the same.

2. Draw it. Exactly what comes, even if it's a tangle of wire.

3. Ask the drawing. “And what kind of wire is that wire?” Add what you see.

4. Use the Compass. “And when this wire is here… what would I like to have happen?” Draw the answer on the other side of the page.

5. Find the connection. Look at both. “And what needs to happen for this wire to become that thing?”

You are your own guide. The drawing holds the map still long enough for you to look at it.

In the literature, this is Clean Self-Modelling.

5

Three rules to keep

1. Don't put your advice in their world. Their words, not yours.

2. Trust the engine. It solves the problem; you keep the map steady.

3. Leave it better than you found it. Nothing added, nothing taken away.

That's the method. The rest is practice.
