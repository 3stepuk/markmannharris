Module 4

On Your Own

When the map is yours, and there is nobody to ask you the question.

When it's you

You can guide someone else. Then you're the one with the heavy rock in your stomach, and there is no one in the room to ask.

Trying to sort it out by sitting still and thinking usually makes it worse. The thinking goes round the same loop it is trying to escape.

So get the map out of your head and into the room. Five ways, in order of difficulty. The first one has nothing wrong in it at all, and it is the one to learn first.

1

Find a place that holds

For the days when nothing is wrong.

Most people only open the map when something has gone wrong. That is like only looking at a game when it crashes — you never learn what it looks like when it works.

You need nothing. You can do this on a bus.

1. Pick the place. Somewhere steady — a bench, a shed, the smell of a car, the top of the stairs. Not because it's nice. Because you chose it.

2. Give it a shape. “And what kind of place is that place?” A light, a colour, a sound, a smell. A place you can see is a place you can find again.

3. Find it in your body. “And where do I feel that place?” Chest, hands, feet, shoulders. When you know where it lands, you know when it has loaded.

4. Find the way in. “And what happens just before I get there?” A door, a smell, a voice at the gate. That is the handle.

5. Keep it. “And what would help me find my way back?” A word, a smell, a picture. Something small enough to carry.

That is the whole thing, and it takes ten minutes. You now have somewhere to stand, whether or not anything is ever wrong. Module 0 walks through the same five steps with a friend.

In the literature, this is Modelling a Resource Space.

2

Draw it

For the everyday loop.

When your thoughts are a tangle, drawing them is faster than thinking about them. You don't need to be good at it. Scribbles work better than neat drawings.

You need: a sheet of paper and something to draw with.

1. Find the thing. “When I'm worried, angry or stuck about [X]… what is that like?”

2. Draw it. The first thing that comes, on the left of the page. If it's a black storm cloud, draw a black storm cloud.

3. Ask the drawing. “And what kind of storm cloud is that?” Add what you see — purple lightning, maybe.

4. Use the Compass. “And when this storm cloud is here… what would I like to have happen?”

5. Draw the answer on the right of the page. A warm sun, perhaps.

6. Find the connection. “And what needs to happen for this storm cloud to become that sun?”

7. Draw what comes in the middle. Whatever connects them. Watch what the page does.

In the literature, this is Clean Self-Modelling through drawing.

3

Use the floor

For the problem too big to hold in your head.

You need a bigger screen. Your bedroom floor will do, with a pack of sticky notes.

1. Name the pieces. Write each part of the problem on its own note — “maths homework”, “want to play”, “tired”.

2. Place them. Stand in the middle of the room. “Where does this belong?” Put it exactly where your gut says.

3. Walk the map. Move around the room and look at it from different places.

4. Change the angle. Stand somewhere odd — on the bed, in the doorway. “And from up here… what do I know now about all of these?”

5. Move the pieces. “Does anything need to move?” Let your hands move them. When they all feel right, you will know what to do next.

In the literature, this is the Clean Space Protocol.

4

Use your two hands

For the trap with two sides.

“I want to go to the sleepover, but I'm scared I'll get homesick.” That is a Glitch Trap, and your system is pulling in two directions at once.

You need: your hands.

1. Load the first hand. Left hand out. “This hand is wanting to go.” Notice how it feels. Heavy? Light? Hot?

2. Load the second. Right hand out. “This hand is being scared of getting homesick.” Notice that one.

3. Hold both. Out in front of you, like two invisible boxes. Look between them.

4. Look at the middle. Stop looking at your hands. Look at the empty space between them. “And what is between this hand and that hand?”

5. Let it work. Don't force anything. Your hands may move on their own — together, apart, turning over. When they stop, the trap has unlocked.

In the literature, this is Somatic Double-Bind Resolution.

5

Build the door

For taking it with you.

You worked it out. The heavy rock has become something lighter. But how do you remember to bring it to school tomorrow?

You need: your imagination, and two minutes.

1. Find the resource. Focus on the good, strong or calm feeling you have now.

2. Ask the question. “And as I take this with me… what is the very first thing I will notice when I walk in tomorrow?”

3. Place it. Imagine walking in with it. What does it look like? How do you walk?

4. Stop. Take a breath. You have planted it in tomorrow. When you walk in, it will be there.

In the literature, this is Self-Future Pacing.

6

Three things to remember

Your system is smart. Even when you feel completely stuck, it knows how to solve it if you give it something to work with.

You don't have to force it. If it isn't moving, leave it on the paper and come back later.

It's your map. Nobody else gets to decide what belongs on it.
