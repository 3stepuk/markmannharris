Module 8 · for adults

The Facilitator's Guide

Teaching notes for practitioners: how to teach young people to facilitate each other and themselves.

What you are doing here

You already know autogenic metaphor, psychoactive landscapes and systemic modelling. You can track a vector and map a double bind. None of that is the job on this page.

The job is to teach young people to do it for each other, and for themselves. You are not facilitating them. You are training a room full of facilitators.

One thing to get right before anything else. These are not small children with a simplified method. Thirteen to sixteen is the age at which the full model becomes usable — they can hold an abstraction, track a structure and notice their own assumptions. What they cannot tolerate is being talked down to. The failure mode in this age group is not comprehension. It is being patronised, and then quietly leaving.

So: full model, adult register, no baby talk. If you find yourself simplifying the idea, you have probably simplified the wrong thing.

1

Three shifts in how you teach it

Model the model

Keep the No Littering rule while you teach. If someone suggests an odd way to use the Magnifying Glass, don't correct them with your map. Ask: “And when you use it that way, what happens to the thing you're looking at?”

Move

Adults will sit in a chair for an hour. Teenagers will not, and shouldn't. Get them on their feet early and use the room often — it is a tool in the method, not a break from it.

Expect the advice monster

The hardest thing to stop is the urge to fix. At this age the empathy is real and it comes out as advice. Give them the honest version: advice is you writing on their map. Their job is to hold the pen still and let the other person write.

2

Module by module

Module 0 — the ground floor

The goal: model something before anyone brings a difficulty. Nothing at this level needs a problem.

Three worlds, one dog. Ask three people about the same thing — a dog, the sea, a thunderstorm — and put the answers side by side. None is wrong; none is the dog. Say that out loud, then send them to collect three more. The exercise isn't the point. The discovery is.

Nobody's is wrong, including yours. The instinct you will fight all day is praise. “What a lovely Safe Zone” is a verdict, and a young person who hears it learns to produce what gets praised. Say back what they gave you, and stop. If they choose something you wouldn't have chosen, that is the material.

Choose, don't offer. Don't hand them a menu of pleasant places. Ask “And what would you like to explore?” and then be quiet for longer than is comfortable. Whatever they say first is the place, even when it sounds ordinary.

Everything in Module 1 runs on this material. The exact-words drill, the Body Clues, the syntax — practise them on a place that holds before you bring a difficulty. Someone who can map a kitchen can map a worry. Someone who has only ever mapped a worry has learned that this is what you do to problems.

Module 1 — the basics

The goal: locate something in the body and ask the six questions without adding content.

The camera angle, taught physically. Hold a hand an inch from your nose, then at arm's length. Same hand. The distance is what changed. That is what a question does.

Body Clues. They are observant but rarely look for breathing and posture. Pair them up: one talks about something they love while the other only watches the body. Calibrate the eye before adding the questions.

The exact-words drill. A says a sentence — “I've got a heavy rock in my stomach.” B must ask a question using only those words. Change “stomach” to “tummy” and a buzzer goes off. Make non-contamination a game of precision, not a rule you nag about.

Module 2 — structure and traps

The goal: move from one landmark to the relationships between them, and to the trap.

The strings, physically. Use a ball of yarn between objects in the room. “And when this chair moves, what happens to the table?” Then map it back to what they described in their own world.

The Glitch Trap. The most useful thing you will teach, and the one that needs the most groundwork. Do not let them skip the middle. Enforce the pause — “And what is between this hand and that hand?” — and let them sit in the silence until something moves.

Letting it settle. They will want to move on the second it feels better. Frame the silence as loading, not awkwardness: when the other person smiles or breathes out, wait ten seconds before the next question.

Module 3 — the long view

The goal: carrying a change into real life, and doing it alone.

The door back. Without it, nothing survives the corridor. Have them physically walk through a doorway holding the new resource, imagining it is the door to the classroom.

Drawing it out. This age can hold a complex metaphor, but drawing externalises it and removes the pressure to perform. The drawing need not be good. If they stall, prompt them to ask their own drawing a question.

3

Common situations

“My friend's metaphor is silly — it's from a game.”

The situation. Someone says their anxiety looks like a character from a game or a film.

The move. Don't read it as not taking it seriously. Pop culture is native architecture for this age. Take it completely cleanly: “And what kind of [that character] is that?” The structure — threat, weight, speed — is encoded in the image whatever the casing.

“I can't see anything. It's blank.”

The situation. Cognitive overload, and the landscape shuts down.

The move. Use the room. Have them stand and move to a different corner. “And from here… what do you know now about that blank map?”

When it gets too big

The situation. A peer conversation uncovers something genuinely heavy.

The move. Teach the protocol before they need it, and make it explicit: a facilitator's job is not to hold a crisis. If someone becomes very distressed, or discloses harm, or talks about hurting themselves, the session stops. Use the camera angle to bring them back to the room — “And where are you sitting right now?” — and bring in an adult. Stopping is not failure. It is the method working.

This is the one part of the programme that is not a game, and it should be taught in those words.

4

The resources

The Field Worksheet. Use it as scaffolding for the first peer practice. Filling in the blanks slows the pace and makes leading questions harder to ask.

The Logbook. Read the conversations aloud, and have them name which question did the work in each line. Treat it like analysing a match, not like reading a textbook.

The thinking underneath. If you want the ground under the method — the lineage, the four principles, and an honest account of what is and isn't known — see The philosophy, in plain language.

Last thing

Your greatest asset is their precision, not their imagination. They already imagine well. What you are giving them is a discipline: a way to stay out of someone else's world while still being useful in it.

Trust the system as much as you would with a paying adult client. When you step back and let them ask the questions, the elegance of what they find will surprise you.
