For parents

What to say when they're struggling

Four rules and six questions. Written so that your teenager can read it too — and they should.

First, the honest bit

When a teenager is struggling, almost every instinct points the same way: fix it. Say the right thing, find the answer, make it better before tea. That instinct is love. It also doesn't work as well as the thing that does.

This page is about the thing that does. It is a way of talking that lets them find their own way out, with you beside them rather than in front of them.

It is not therapy. It is not a diagnosis. It is not a promise that they will stop worrying or hurting. It is a set of habits — four rules and six questions — that work in a kitchen, in a car, or on a walk.

Nothing here needs to be run on them. That is the point of the next section.

1

Why this is different at 13–16

With a younger child, you can usually ask and they will answer. Somewhere around thirteen, that changes. They stop reporting, and start choosing what to tell you. That is not a malfunction. It is the developmental job of the age.

The research on this is fairly consistent, and it is uncomfortable: monitoring shrinks disclosure. Checking, tracking and asking too closely tends to reduce what they tell you, while warmth and autonomy tend to keep it open. You get more by being the person they can talk to than by being the person who checks.

Which gives three ways this goes wrong, and all three are common:

Surveillance. Anything that positions you as the inspector of their inner life. It reduces the very access it is trying to get.

Shut out. “It's private” with nothing offered, which leaves you filling the gap with worst-case guesses.

Handing them the technique. Clean questions from a parent can land as an interview. “What kind of worried is that worried?” asked by someone with authority is a different act from the same question asked by a friend.

So the frame is this: if they invite you in, here's how to listen. Not here's how to run this on your teenager.

2

A question beats advice

When someone is upset, the advice we give is our map of their problem, not theirs. “Just ignore them” comes out of our head. It usually assumes we have understood the problem, and often we haven't.

Advice also lands as a correction. It tells them their own attempt wasn't good enough. Enough of that and they stop bringing things to you at all.

Two ways to answer the same sentence. They say: “I don't want to go in tomorrow.” You can say “you'll be fine, it's always better than you think” — that's you answering. Or “what is it about tomorrow?” — that's them answering.

A good question doesn't hand them a map. It turns the light on so they can read the one they already have. And because the answer comes out of their own head, it stays.

There is a second reason, and it matters more. When someone names a feeling precisely — “a tight knot”, “like a wire pulled too far” — something loosens just by being looked at. The naming is part of the fixing. You don't have to be clever. You have to ask, and then be quiet.

And none of the six questions below begins with “why”. “Why” asks for an explanation; a clean question asks them to look at the thing itself.

3

The four rules

1. Don't litter their world

Use their words, not yours. If they say “I've got a knot in my stomach”, don't come back with “is it a big knot?” or “maybe it's nerves”. “Big” is your word. “Nerves” is your word. Stay with “knot”.

2. Change the camera angle

When a feeling is too much, they are standing too close to it. Your job isn't to make it smaller; it's to help them step back far enough to look at it. “Where is that knot?” does it, because now they are looking at it from outside instead of being inside it.

3. The map is live

Their inner world changes while you look at it. The knot may melt, move, or turn into something else the moment you ask. That isn't them messing about. Go with it, and trust what they report.

4. Don't rush the fix

The moment they feel a bit better, the instinct is to wrap it up — “good, sorted”. Don't. When their shoulders drop or they take a breath, that is the shift landing. Sit in the quiet and let it settle. Rushing to the tidy ending undoes the thing that just happened.

4

The six questions

You only need six. They all work the same way: take their exact words, drop them in the gap, and wait.

Ask one, then be quiet. The silence is where the answer comes from. If you fill it, you are back to being the one who talks.

1. “What kind of [X] is that [X]?” — turns a blurry feeling into a thing. “I feel sick.” → “What kind of sick is that sick?” → “Like my stomach's full of butterflies.”

2. “Where is [X]?” — pins it to a spot. “Where's that butterflies feeling?” → “Right in my middle.”

3. “Is there anything else about [X]?” — looks around the edges. → “They're going round and round.”

4. “What happens just before [X]?” — finds the trigger. → “When I think about tomorrow.”

5. “And then what happens?” — sees where it leads. → “I can't eat.”

6. “When [X] is there, what would you like to have happen?” — points them towards the way out. → “I want them to settle down.”

The last one is the most useful and the easiest to forget. It doesn't solve anything by itself. It turns their head from the problem to the direction they want to go.

5

One conversation

A test tomorrow. They are fifteen, and they are not in the mood for this — which is normal, and is part of the point.

Teenager

“My stomach feels weird about the test.”

You

“Your stomach feels weird.”Just repeat it. Wait.

You

“What kind of weird is that weird?”

Teenager

“Like there's a tight knot in it.”

You

“A tight knot. And where is that knot?”

Teenager

“Right in my middle.”

You

“And is there anything else about the knot?”

Teenager

“It gets tighter when I think about everyone finishing before me.”

You

“And when there's a tight knot and you think about everyone finishing first, what would you like to have happen?”

Teenager

“I want it to be loose. Like a bit of string instead of a knot.”

You

“And what would need to happen for the knot to be like a bit of string?”

Teenager

“Maybe if I did one past paper tonight. I know more than I think.”

You

“And can the knot be like a bit of string then?”

Teenager

“Yeah. It's looser already.”A breath. Then nothing.

You

Says nothing. Lets it settle.

Notice what didn't happen. No “you'll be fine”, no offer to test them, no “don't worry”. Just their words, and a question. They found the way to practise on their own.

6

The advice monster

Every parent has a voice that wants to jump in with the answer. “Just relax.” “Everyone feels like that.” “You'll be fine.” It means well. It is also the single thing that most often gets in the way.

It fires because we can't bear to watch them struggle when we can see the fix. But the fix we can see is ours. Hand it over and we teach them that the answers live in our head.

The replacement is simple. When the urge hits, do two things: say their words back, and ask one question. “A tight knot… and where is that knot?”

When it slips out — and it will — you haven't wrecked anything. Catch it and ask a question behind it. “You'll be fine… and what would you like to have happen?” The question is the reset.

7

What if they won't play along?

Some won't hand you a metaphor. “What kind of sad is that sad?” gets a shrug: “Dunno.”

That's fine. It isn't a test. Drop the question and stay with them. “You just feel sad.” Repeating their words, on its own, does more than most of the advice we hand out.

The questions are a tool, not a script. If the tool doesn't fit today, put it down and sit there.

Pick the moment, too. Tired, cross or hungry is not the moment. A car is often better than a kitchen, because nobody has to make eye contact.

8

When to stop

Stop when it's getting bigger, not smaller. If they are becoming more distressed, you have gone far enough. The aim is never to dig to the bottom in one go.

Stop kindly. You don't need a reason. “That's enough for tonight. We'll come back to it.” Or bring them back to the room: “And where are you sitting right now?” — the mind returns from the feeling to the chair under them.

Bring someone else in when it is too big for a parent. If the fear or sadness doesn't shift, or it is about something that worries you — something at school, talk of hurting themselves, a weight that has been there a long time — this is not the tool for it. Talk to their GP, the school, or someone trained. A good guide knows the difference between a bad day and a heavy load, and a heavy load is not yours to carry alone.

9

How to start

The easiest first go isn't a trouble at all. Before you use any of this on something that hurts, use it once on a place that holds. Ask them to pick somewhere — a kitchen, a bench, the car on the way home — not because it's nice, because they chose it. Then ask the six questions about that.

It is the same skill on easy ground, and it teaches you both something useful: what their world looks like when nothing is wrong with it. You also end up with somewhere to stand.

Keep the first go small. One question is a win. The Compass — “what would you like to have happen?” — is the one to keep in your pocket. Two or three questions, then stop, whether or not anything seemed to change. It often changes later, out of sight.
