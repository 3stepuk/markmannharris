For adults · the thinking underneath

The philosophy, in plain language

Where Clean Language came from, what it rests on, and honest guesses about why it might work.

What this is

The Secret World is a programme for young people. The parent's guide is a handful of habits for adults. This page is the floor underneath both of them — the ideas they rest on, and where those ideas came from.

It is not a scientific paper, and it does not claim that any of this is proven. It is an honest account of a way of thinking about the mind, written for a reader with no training who wants to understand it.

The prose is British English. Quotations keep the source's own spelling.

Where it came from

The line runs from one man working alone in the 1980s, through two people who named what he did, to a third who carried it out of the therapy room and into classrooms and workplaces.

David Grove was a therapist in New Zealand. He noticed something most practitioners of his day would have tidied away: when a person talked about a difficult experience, they did not say “I feel anxious”. They said “it's like I'm standing on the edge of a cliff”, or “there's a tight band around my chest”. The mind, left to itself, speaks in pictures and places. Grove stopped correcting that and started following it.

By asking a handful of tightly shaped questions using only the person's own words, he found that the system did something remarkable:

“By asking highly constrained questions using their exact words, the system could organically self-correct.” — Grove, quoted in the working notes

The Secret World is a translation of this insight into a game. An evaluation of that translation put it plainly: it “stripped away dense, academic jargon without losing the core epistemological rigour of David Grove's work.” (David Grove Text Evaluation.txt)

James Lawley and Penny Tompkins came next. Grove worked by instinct; they watched what he actually did and turned it into a method that could be taught. They counted the questions and found that only a dozen really carried the weight.

“Reverse-engineered Grove's intuitive genius into Symbolic Modelling, codifying the vast array of inquiries into 12 core questions and a replicable 5-Stage Therapeutic Process.” — Grove, quoted in the working notes

Caitlin Walker then took the method out of the one-to-one room. If it worked on one person's inner map, she reasoned, it should work on a team's, a classroom's, a whole organisation's. She gave the wider version its own name.

“Expanded the model from 1-on-1 clinical therapy into Systemic Modelling, applying the syntax to groups, education, and organizations, and documenting the real-time cognitive skills required to facilitate it.” — Grove, quoted in the working notes

Three names, one movement: Grove discovered it, Lawley and Tompkins named it, Walker spread it. The teenager at the kitchen table and the 1980s clinic are doing the same thing — asking clean questions in the person's own words, and trusting the system to sort itself out.

Four guiding principles

Underneath the method sit four ideas. Each has a name in the field and a plain-English version.

1. The person's system knows how to sort itself out. (Client autonomy.) You are not fixing a broken machine. You are standing beside a system that, given the right conditions, heals and organises itself. The facilitator's job is to provide the conditions, not the answers.

“The client's system inherently knows how to heal and organize itself.” — working notes, after Lawley & Tompkins

2. The symptom carries its own solution. (The coded solution.) The very thing that is wrong holds the clue to what is right. A tight knot is not noise to be explained away — it is information, and the way out is already inside it.

“The symptom, double-bind, or negative feeling contains its own encoded solution.” — working notes, after Lawley & Tompkins

3. Stay out of their landscape. (The ecology of the landscape.) Whatever world the person describes, you enter it on their terms and leave it exactly as you found it. You do not add your own furniture.

“The facilitator must enter the client's map without ‘rearranging the furniture.' Zero external importation.” — working notes, after Lawley & Tompkins

4. Midwife the information, not the ego. (Information-centred.) Your attention is on what is emerging, not on being liked, or clever, or right. That is a hard ask, and it is the whole craft in a sentence.

“The facilitator acts as a midwife to the information, not a companion to the client's ego.” — working notes, after Lawley & Tompkins

What “symbolic modelling” means

“Symbolic” here does not mean “metaphorical, as in not quite real”. It means nearly the opposite. When a person says their worry is “a heavy iron blanket”, the model treats the blanket as the real object of work. You do not translate the blanket back into “anxiety” and then work on “anxiety”. You work on the blanket — because that is where the mind has put the feeling, and the mind tends to cooperate with its own filing system.

The definition the whole thing rests on:

“Clean Language is a methodology for exploring the subjective, metaphorical structure of human experience without introducing the facilitator's own assumptions.” — Grove, quoted in the working notes

The word “subjective” is doing a lot of work there. This is not an attempt to find the objective truth of a situation. It is an attempt to map one person's inner landscape, on the understanding that their map is the only one in play.

So why not translate? Two reasons. The first is that swapping “blanket” for “anxiety” is exactly the importation the method forbids — you have replaced their word with yours, and the moment you do, you are no longer in their world. The second is deeper. The abstract word is harder to work with, not easier.

“Abstract nominalizations (like ‘anxiety') must be explored cleanly until they become tangible, physical metaphors (e.g., ‘a heavy iron blanket').” — working notes

A vague noun is a fog. A specific picture is a handle. The whole method is the discipline of getting from the fog to the handle without ever supplying the handle yourself.

Why the questions look the way they do

Read a clean question and it looks almost broken: “And what kind of tight is that tight?” The repetition, the “and”, the present tense — none of it is accident.

The exact words. The questioner repeats the person's own phrase instead of replacing it. That is the non-contamination rule made visible: their words, not yours, because their words are the only reliable coordinates into their world.

The “and”. Each question opens with a soft “and”, not a full stop. It signals that nothing is finished — we are still on the same map, walking together. The field's own description of the fuller rhythm is “the hypnotic, 3-part pacing (‘And… and as…')”.

The present tense. “What kind of tight is that tight?”, never “what kind was it?” The feeling is treated as here and now, still in the room. It is not a memory to be reported; it is a live landscape to be looked at.

The waiting. After the question comes the hardest part — silence. The answer takes time to arrive, because it is not being recalled. It is being found.

Here is the method's own worked example of the syntax, what it calls the full form:

“Repeat their exact words + ‘And when…' + Ask the Key. Example: ‘And you feel a knot… and when you feel a knot… whereabouts is that knot?'” — The Twelve Keys

The repetition pins the feeling to a place. The “and” joins the questions into one continuous movement. The “whereabouts” opens the map. Nothing in the sentence is decoration; every word is load-bearing.

How it might be working

This is the honest section. No one can prove why clean questions change people. What follows are guesses — four of them, each worth no more than the word “might”. Where a guess reaches past what the sources actually say, it is marked [VERIFY].

Attention and embodiment. The simplest guess. A clean question takes a feeling that is everywhere and nowhere — “I'm anxious” — and asks the person to point at it. Attention lands on a specific place in the body, and the body responds. Mark's own field notes put it plainly: “Naming a sensation aloud appears to regulate emotional intensity.” (Neuro-Symbolic Phenomenology — Voice and Regulation, “Key Observations”) That is a hypothesis, not a fact — but it is the hypothesis the practice stands on.

Memory and re-consolidation. [VERIFY] A second guess reaches into how memory works. The idea, current in some corners of neuroscience, is that a memory is not a fixed recording: each time it is recalled it becomes briefly open to change before it is stored again. If that is so, revisiting a difficult moment inside a calm, structured exchange might let it be stored back a little differently.

“repeatedly telling and refining the story… could modify how that story is encoded in the brain – potentially reducing its emotional charge.” — Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, 2020

The step from that sentence to “re-consolidation” is mine, not the source's — hence the marker.

Rumination and the default mode network. The best-sourced of the four. The default mode network is a set of brain regions that hum when the mind is turned inward — remembering, daydreaming, telling itself the story of itself. In low mood it can get stuck on, spinning the same loop. The technical word is rumination — “the repetitive, passive focus on negative thoughts and feelings”. The guess is that a clean question interrupts the loop by turning the inward chatter outward: into spoken words, a felt place in the body, a picture in front of the eyes.

“Voice interrupts the loop of rumination — the act of speaking finalises, externalises, and dissolves it.” — Neuro-Symbolic Phenomenology — Voice and Regulation, “Key Observations”

Still a guess — but a guess with something behind it.

Expectation and prediction. [VERIFY] The fourth guess is that the question itself is the medicine, whatever the content. Ask “what would you like to have happen?” and you have pointed the system somewhere. The Secret World names this directly: the map is live, and your questions make the world move. The further idea — that the brain is always predicting, and a well-placed question simply redirects the prediction — is a modern frame the sources do not make. I have added it, so it carries the marker.

Mark's own hypothesis. There is one more, and it gets its own label because it is not borrowed — it is his. Over the last while Mark has been developing something he calls neuro-symbolic phenomenology. His own definition:

“A living research practice that treats the subjective experience of symbolic interaction as both data and method.” — Mark Mann-Harris, NSP working notes (unpublished)

And a second line, just as important: “NSP is not therapy.” His working hypothesis, in one sentence, is that how we use language and symbol is not a report on an already-fixed self — it is how the self is continuously made, and that paying close attention to that making, especially by speaking aloud, changes it. It is his hypothesis, not settled science, and he would be the first to say so.

A last word

Three names, four principles, a definition, a syntax, and a shelf of guesses. The thread that runs through all of it is one line, and it is the best place to end:

“Stop fighting the other person's brain, and instead, work with its natural filing system.” — working notes
