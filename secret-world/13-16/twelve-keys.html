The Twelve Keys

The twelve questions the whole method rests on. Learn two of them properly and you have most of it.

Twelve, not a hundred

Talking about a problem is not just telling a story. The words are the map. When someone describes a feeling, they are rendering something that was not visible a moment ago.

You don't need a hundred questions for that. You need twelve, and most conversations use two or three.

Before you use them on anything difficult, use two of them on a place that holds: Key 2 (the Magnifying Glass) and Key 10 (the Compass) on a bench or a kitchen. That is Module 0, and it takes ten minutes. It will teach you more than reading this page twice.

Group 1

Building the map

Form and space. These turn a blurry feeling into something you can look at.

Key 1 · The Flashlight

“And is there anything else about [X]?”

It lights the edges of a landmark instead of moving to a new part of the map. It is usually where the detail you haven't heard yet is hiding.

Key 2 · The Magnifying Glass

“And what kind of [X] is that [X]?”

It turns “I'm scared” into something specific: “an icy block”. Once it is an object, the system knows what to do with it.

Key 3 · The Map Pin

“And where is [X]?”

A feeling that is everywhere is hard to look at. A pin gives it coordinates — “in my throat” — and that alone changes things.

Key 4 · The Radar Scan

“And whereabouts?”

A softer Map Pin. If it is “in my chest”, whereabouts invites a scan of the area for the exact spot.

Key 5 · The Shape-Shifter

“And that's [X] like what?”

It turns a physical feeling into an object. “My stomach hurts” becomes “like a washing machine spinning”. The washing machine is easier to work with.

Group 2

Time

Sequence and backstory. These track the timeline without ever asking “why?” — because “why” invites an explanation, and an explanation is not the map.

Key 6 · The Play Button

“And then what happens?”

Moves it forward one step. The immediate next thing.

Key 7 · The Fast-Forward

“And what happens next?”

The same idea, run longer, to see the bigger consequence.

Key 8 · The Rewind

“And what happens just before [X]?”

The tracker. It walks back to the moment before the trap closed, so the trigger becomes visible.

Key 9 · The Origin Tracker

“And where could [X] come from?”

It looks past the trigger to the source. “That heavy rock came from a deep, dark cave underground.”

Group 3

Changing it

Direction and upgrades. These don't just look at the map; they help the system write something new.

Key 10 · The Compass

“And what would you like to have happen?”

It stops the staring and turns them towards a direction. It is the single most useful question in the twelve.

A variation worth knowing: ask the landmark itself. “And what would that heavy rock like to have happen?” Sometimes the rock wants to be a pebble.

Key 11 · The Blueprint

“And what needs to happen for [X] to [Y]?”

Once there is a direction, this asks for the steps. It maps the thread between what is and what they want.

Key 12 · The System Check

“And can [X] [Y]?” — e.g. “And can that rock turn into a pebble?”

It tests whether the change is available. If the body relaxes and they say yes, it is. If they hold their breath and say no, go back to the Flashlight and find more that holds first.

Group 4

How to ask

Delivery matters as much as the question.

The full form

When someone is very stuck or upset, use the full, repetitive form. It slows everything down and keeps the map steady.

Repeat their exact words + “And when…” + the question.

“And you feel a knot… and when you feel a knot… whereabouts is that knot?”

The everyday form

In a normal conversation, the full form sounds like a robot. A short bridge is fine, as long as you keep their words.

“So there's a knot… whereabouts is it?”

It sounds like a conversation. It is still doing the work.

Where to start

You don't need all twelve. If you only ever use the Magnifying Glass — “what kind of [X]?” — and the Compass — “what would you like to have happen?” — you are already better at this than most adults.

Keep the sheet. The next time someone's map goes wrong, you'll know which question to ask.
