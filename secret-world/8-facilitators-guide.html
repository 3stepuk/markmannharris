Module 8 · For adults

The Facilitator's Guide

Teaching notes for advanced Clean Language practitioners.

As a Clean Language practitioner you already understand autogenic metaphor, psychoactive landscapes and systemic modelling. You know how to track a vector and map a double bind.

Your objective here is different. You are not facilitating the children; you are teaching the children how to facilitate each other and themselves. You are training the next generation of systemic modellers.

This age group — eight to twelve — is uniquely primed for this work. Developmentally they are moving from concrete to abstract thinking. They intuitively understand world-building, game engines and coding because they have grown up in digital environments like Minecraft and Roblox. To them, a “psychoactive landscape” is just a live-rendering server.

1. Core pedagogical shifts

Model the model

Embody the “No Littering” rule while teaching. If a child suggests a bizarre way to use the Magnifying Glass, do not correct them with your map. Ask: “And when you use it that way, what happens to the glitch?”

Kinesthetic > auditory

Adults can process Clean Language sitting in a chair for an hour. Pre-teens cannot. Get them out of their seats as quickly as possible, and use the Teleporter Pad early and often to manage their physical energy.

The advice monster

The hardest thing for an eleven-year-old is not to give advice. Their empathy manifests as trying to fix their friend. Reinforce that giving advice is like hacking your friend's game and breaking their save file.

2. Module breakdown and teaching strategies

Level 0 — the play stage

The goal: get them modelling before they meet anything difficult. Nothing at this level needs a problem.

Three worlds, one dog

Ask three children about the same thing — a dog, the sea, a thunderstorm — and put the three answers side by side. None of them is wrong, and none of them is the dog. Say that out loud, then send them off to collect three more. The exercise is not the point; the discovery is. The person next to them is walking around inside a world they have never seen.

Nobody's is wrong, including yours

The instinct you will have to fight all day is praise. “What a lovely safe zone” is a verdict, and a child who hears it learns to produce what gets praised. Say back what they gave you, and stop there. If they choose something you would not have chosen, that is the material. You are not there to like it.

Choose, don't offer

Do not hand them a menu of pleasant places. Ask “And what would you like to explore?” and then be quiet for longer than is comfortable. Whatever they say first is the place, even when it sounds ordinary — a bench, a nan's kitchen, the smell of a car.

Everything in Level 1 runs on this material. The exact-words drill, the Body Clues, the three-part syntax — practise them on a place that holds before you bring them to a symptom. A child who can map a kitchen can map a worry. A child who has only ever mapped a worry has learned that this is what you do to problems.

Level 1 — the basics

The goal: locate a somatic node and ask three-part syntax questions without adding content. By now they have done it on a Safe Zone; Level 1 is the same skill, held to the same standard.

Introducing the Camera Angle

Kids get easily overwhelmed by peer drama or school stress. Teach it practically: have them hold a hand an inch from their nose, then at arm's length. Explain that Clean Questions naturally push the monster to arm's length so it can be studied safely.

Body Clues

Kids are observant but rarely look for micro-muscle shifts or breathing changes. Pair them up: one talks about a hobby while the other only watches breathing and posture. Train their visual calibration before introducing the questions.

The “Exact Words” drill

Child A says a sentence — “I have a heavy rock in my tummy.” Child B must ask a question using only those words. If B changes “tummy” to “stomach”, sound a buzzer. Make non-contamination a high-stakes game of precision.

Level 2 — systemic maps and double binds

The goal: move from single-node exploration to mapping relationships, vectors and binds. This is the first level that needs a difficulty in it, and it needs the six questions fluent first. Difficulty is a later section, not the ground floor — the way you learn to climb with ropes before you climb.

Finding the Invisible Strings

Kids view problems in isolation. Use physical props — a ball of yarn connecting items in the room — then ask: “And when this chair moves, what happens to the table?” Map the exercise back to their internal landmarks.

The Glitch Trap

The most profound tool you will teach, and the one that needs the most groundwork. Pre-teens face double binds daily. Do not let them skip the middle space. Enforce the pause — “And what is between [hand one] and [hand two]?” — and let them stare at the empty space until the nervous system shifts.

The Autosave

Kids want to rush to the next level the second they feel better. Frame silence not as awkward but as downloading: “When your friend smiles or takes a breath, freeze for ten seconds to let their brain save the game.”

Level 3 — future pacing and self-modelling

The goal: ecological integration and agency over their own pathways.

The Portal Generator

Without this, the changes won't survive the schoolyard. Practise throwing the shield through the portal: have them physically walk through a doorway in the training room holding their new resource, imagining it is the door to their classroom.

The Doodle Quest

This age group can struggle to hold a complex metaphor in working memory. Drawing externalises it. The drawing need not be good art — stick figures and scribbles process subcortical data just as well. If they get stuck, prompt them to ask their own drawing the Clean Questions.

3. Handling common “kid” dynamics

“My friend's metaphor is silly — it's from a game.”

The scenario. A child says their anxiety looks like “Darth Vader riding a Pikachu.”

The move. Do not read this as them not taking it seriously. To a child, pop culture is native architecture. Validate it completely cleanly: “And what kind of Pikachu is that Pikachu?” The systemic structure — threat, weight, speed — is perfectly encoded in that image, whatever the casing.

“I don't see anything. My map is blank.”

The scenario. A child hits cognitive overload and the landscape shuts down.

The move. Use Clean Space. Have them physically stand and move to a different corner of the room. Ask: “And from here… what do you know now about that blank map?”

“I want to play Co-Op, but my friend is crying.”

The scenario. A peer session uncovers genuine trauma or high-intensity emotional load.

The move. Establish a Red Flag / Log Off protocol. Teach them that Master Explorers know when a level is too dangerous for a kid to play alone. If a friend becomes highly distressed, the Explorer's job is to use the Camera Angle to zoom out — “And from where you are sitting in this chair, what do you know about that feeling?” — save the game, and bring in an adult Administrator.

4. Deploying the resources

The Field Worksheet

Use it as training wheels. During the first peer-to-peer practice, require them to physically fill in the blanks. It slows their pacing and prevents leading questions.

The Logbook

Read the missions aloud as a group. Have the kids identify exactly which tool the Master Explorer used in each line. Treat it like analysing game footage of a professional esports match.

The thinking underneath

If you want the ground under the method — the lineage, the four principles, and an honest account of what is and isn't known about why it works — see The philosophy, in plain language.

Final thought

Your greatest asset is their imagination. You are giving them a linguistic framework to interact directly with their own neurology. Trust their inner engine just as fiercely as you would a high-paying adult client. When you step back and let them ask the questions, you will be astounded by the elegance of their self-organising systems.
