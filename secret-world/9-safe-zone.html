Module 0

The Safe Zone

The quiet, strong place on your map — and the discovery that nobody else's world looks quite like yours.

1. Everyone's world is different

Try this before you try anything else.

Ask three people about the same thing. A dog, the sea, a thunderstorm. Then listen to how far apart the answers are.

“A dog”

“Warm and heavy. He leans on your leg.”

“A dog”

“Loud. All teeth and cold nose.”

“A dog”

“Soft ears. Like a scarf you can hold.”

Three people. One dog. Three completely different worlds.

None of them is wrong, and none of them is the dog. Each one is the dog as that person has it. Once you have spotted that, you cannot stop spotting it. The person next to you is walking around inside a world you have never seen.

That is the best thing in this guide. Finding out how differently your friends see the same thing is better than finding a nest of beetles under a log, and it takes less time.

2. The quiet, strong place

Some landmarks hold.

Things on a map move about. A worry grows. A mood changes colour. A thought turns into something else while you are looking at it.

But some landmarks hold. You look at them and they stay. Those are the places where the world goes steady, and where a person can stand. This guide calls them Safe Zones.

Adults call this Resource Space

The Safe Zone

A landmark that holds. A place, a person, a pet, a song, a moment — anything the explorer chooses. Every map has one, and it is not installed by you. It is found.

Adults call this Somatic Endpoints

Body Clues

You know a Safe Zone has been found because the body says so. A big breath out. Shoulders dropping. Eyes going up and away. That is a door unlocking.

Adults call this Non-Contamination

Their choice, not yours

You do not decide whether it is a good place. The explorer picks it, and you follow. If they pick something you would not have picked, that is the material — and it is not yours to correct.

3. How to model it

Five moves. The same six questions you already know.

Move 1 · Choose the place

The explorer picks.

“And what would you like to explore?”

How it works. Do not offer a menu. Wait. Whatever they say first is the place, even if it sounds ordinary — a bench, a nan's kitchen, the smell of a car.

Move 2 · Give it a shape or a sound

Make it findable.

“And what kind of [X] is that [X]?”“And does [X] have a size or a shape?”

How it works. The Magnifying Glass and the Map Pin. A place with a colour, a light or a sound can be found again. A place with nothing on it cannot.

Move 3 · Track the trail

Where it comes from, where it goes.

“And where does [X] come from?”“And what happens just before [X]?”“And then what happens?”

How it works. The Rewind and Fast-Forward buttons. Most Safe Zones have a way in — a door, a smell, a sound at the gate. That way in is the useful part.

Move 4 · Find it in the body

Where it lands.

“And where do you feel [X]?”

How it works. Pin it to a place in the body — chest, feet, hands, throat. This is the Body Clue, and it is how you both know the map has actually moved.

Move 5 · Make a way back

A door handle, not a magic trick.

“And what would help you find your way back to [X]?”

How it works. The Quest Compass. A smell, a word, a sound, a picture. Something small they can carry. It does not have to work every time — it only has to be a handle.

4. Let's play: a real conversation

Here is a ten-year-old modelling a place with a friend. Watch how the Explorer never says “that sounds lovely”, and never adds anything that was not there.

Friend

“My nan's kitchen. That's my place.”A Safe Zone, chosen by the Friend.

Explorer

“And your nan's kitchen. And what kind of kitchen is that kitchen?”The Magnifying Glass.

Friend

“It's small. There's a yellow light on and the kettle's always just boiled.”

Explorer

“A small kitchen, and a yellow light, and the kettle just boiled. And is there anything else about that yellow light?”The Flashlight.

Friend

“It's warm on the ceiling. Like the whole room is glowing a bit.”A shape and a light: it can be found again.

Explorer

“And the whole room is glowing a bit. And where do you feel that glow?”The Map Pin, to the body.

Friend

(Big breath out, shoulders drop) “In my chest. It goes loose.”A Body Clue.

Explorer

“And in your chest, and it goes loose. And what happens just before you get to that kitchen?”The Rewind Button.

Friend

“The front door. You can smell toast before you're even in.”

Explorer

“And you can smell toast before you're even in. And when you're standing in that kitchen, then what happens?”The Fast-Forward Button.

Friend

“I just put my bag down. I don't have to be anything.”

Explorer

“And you put your bag down, and you don't have to be anything. And what would help you find your way back to that kitchen when you're not there?”The Quest Compass — a way back.

Friend

“The smell of toast. I could just think about toast.”

Explorer

“And the smell of toast.”Nothing added. That is the whole craft.

5. When to use it

When nothing is wrong

The easiest time, and the best time. The questions are learned on a place that already holds, so nobody is learning two things at once.

Before something hard

A test, a hospital visit, a new school. Map the place first, so the explorer has somewhere to stand when they get there.

Just to practise

Ten minutes on a bench, a song or a nan's kitchen. Every conversation you have makes the next question easier to ask.

6. A note for the adult

The material in this guide is written for the young person. This page is the one to read first, and the reasons are practical rather than philosophical.

The questions are learned on easy material. Asking “And what kind of kitchen is that kitchen?” about a place that holds is a different job from asking it about something that hurts. Practise the first before you need the second.

The explorer ends up with somewhere to stand. A mapped Safe Zone is a place, with a shape, a body feeling and a way back. It is useful on its own, whether or not anything else is ever modelled.

The variety does the teaching. The discovery in section 1 — that two people can hold the same thing in two completely different worlds — is the point of the exercise, not a warm-up to it.

This framing came from Judy Rees. She suggested that starting with play, and with the sheer variety of how people experience the world, would engage a young person more than starting with difficulty. And that difficulty, when it comes, is better handled as its own separate section — the way you learn to climb with ropes before you climb.

Nothing on this page asks the adult to judge the explorer's material, to call it good, or to steer it. You ask the questions, they choose the answer, and the map does the rest.
