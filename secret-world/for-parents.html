A Parent's Guide to The Secret World

What to say when your child is struggling — and what not to say.

What this is (and isn't)

When a child is struggling, almost every instinct a parent has points the same way: fix it. Say the right thing, find the answer, make it better before tea. There's nothing wrong with that instinct. It's love. It just doesn't work as well as the thing that does.

This guide is about the thing that does. It's a way of talking to a child that lets them find their own way out of a bad feeling, with you beside them instead of in front of them.

It is not therapy. It is not a diagnosis. It is not a promise that your child will stop worrying or hurting. It is a set of simple habits — four rules and six questions — that you can use in a kitchen, at bedtime, in a car.

The child's own version of all this is built as a game called The Secret World, and you can see it at markmannharris.com/secret-world/ if you're curious. You don't need it to use this guide.

Why questions beat advice

When a child is upset, the advice we give is our map of their problem, not theirs. "Just ignore the boy" comes out of our head. It usually assumes we've understood the problem properly, and half the time we haven't.

Advice also lands on a child as a kind of correction. It tells them their own attempt to sort it out wasn't good enough. Enough of that, and a child learns to stop bringing their problems to us at all. They'd rather sit with it than be told, again, what they already tried.

The same moment, two ways. Your child says "I don't want to go to school tomorrow." You can answer with advice — "you'll be fine, it's always better than you think" — or with a question — "and what is it about tomorrow?" The advice is you, answering. The question is them, answering. Every good moment in this guide is the second one.

A good question works the other way round. It doesn't hand them a map. It turns the light on so they can read the map they already have. And because the answer comes out of their own head, it sticks in a way our words rarely do.

There's a second reason, and it matters more. When a child names a feeling in their own words — "a tight knot", "butterflies going round and round" — something in their body starts to loosen just by being looked at. The naming is part of the fixing. You don't have to be clever. You just have to ask.

And this is why it isn't "just talking". The child isn't describing a feeling that stays the same while they describe it. The describing changes the feeling. The more precisely they name it, the more it moves.

The four rules

These are the ground rules. Learn them and you're most of the way there.

1. Don't litter their world.

Use their words, not yours. If your child says "I've got a knot in my tummy", don't come back with "is it a big knot?" or "maybe it's nerves". "Big" is your word. "Nerves" is your word. You've just put bats in their cave. Stay with "knot".

2. Change the camera angle.

When a feeling is scary, the child is standing too close to it — it fills the whole screen. Your job isn't to make the feeling smaller. It's to help them step back far enough to look at it. Asking "where is that knot?" does it, because now they're looking at it from outside instead of being inside it. Hold your hand an inch from your nose, then at arm's length. The hand is the same size. The feeling is the same size too. It's the distance that changes, and distance is what a question can give.

3. The map is live.

Their inner world changes while you look at it. The knot they describe might melt, or move, or turn into something else the moment you ask about it. That's not them messing about. That's the world moving. Go with it. Trust what they report, even when it surprises you.

4. Don't rush the fix.

The moment a child feels a bit better, a parent's instinct is to wrap it up — "good, all sorted." Don't. When their shoulders drop or they take a deep breath, that's the shift landing. Sit in the quiet with them and let it settle. Rushing to the next question or the tidy ending undoes the very thing that just happened.

The six questions

You only need six. They all work the same way: take the child's exact words, drop them in the gap, and wait.

Ask one, then be quiet. The silence is where their answer comes from. If you fill it, you're back to being the one who talks.

1. "What kind of [X] is that [X]?" — turns a blurry feeling into a thing. Kitchen: "I feel sick." → "What kind of sick is that sick?" → "Like my tummy's got butterflies."

2. "Where is [X]?" — pins the thing to a spot. Bedtime: "Where is that butterflies feeling?" → "Right in my middle."

3. "Is there anything else about [X]?" — looks around the edges. Car: "Is there anything else about the butterflies?" → "They're going round and round."

4. "What happens just before [X]?" — finds the trigger. Kitchen: "What happens just before the butterflies start?" → "When I think about school tomorrow."

5. "And then what happens?" — sees where it leads. Bedtime: "And then what happens when they go round and round?" → "I can't eat my tea."

6. "When [X] is there, what would you like to have happen?" — points them toward the way out. Car: "When the butterflies are going round and round, what would you like to have happen?" → "I want them to settle down."

That last one is the most powerful, and the easiest to forget. It doesn't solve anything by itself. It turns the child's head from the problem to the direction they want to go — and children know what they want far more often than we give them credit for.

What if they won't play along?

Some children won't hand you a metaphor. Ask "what kind of sad is that sad?" and they shrug: "I don't know." That's fine. It's not a test. Drop the question and stay with them. "You just feel sad." Repeating their words back, on its own, does more than most of the advice we hand out. The questions are a tool, not a script. If the tool doesn't fit today, put it down and just sit there.

Pick the moment too. If your child is tired, or cross, or hungry, this is not the time. Do it over toast at the kitchen table, or in the car where nobody has to make eye contact. A tired, cross child is not a good subject, and forcing it teaches them that this is an interrogation.

One full conversation

Here's the whole thing put together. Bedtime, a child of about eight, a spelling test tomorrow.

You won't copy it line for line — that's not the point. Watch the shape: repeat, ask, wait, and let the child do the finding.

Child

My tummy feels funny about the spelling test.

Parent

Your tummy feels funny. (Just repeat it back. Wait.)

Parent

What kind of funny is that funny?

Child

Like there's a big tight knot in it.

Parent

A big tight knot. And where is that knot?

Child

Right in my middle. (points)

Parent

And is there anything else about the knot?

Child

It gets tighter when I think about standing up to spell.

Parent

And when there's a tight knot and you think about standing up to spell, what would you like to have happen?

Child

I want it to be loose. Like a bit of string, not a knot.

Parent

And what would need to happen for the knot to be like a bit of string?

Child

Maybe if I practised the words once more. I know them better than I think.

Parent

And can the knot be like a bit of string then?

Child

Yeah. It's a bit looser already. (takes a deep breath)

Parent

(says nothing, lets it settle)

Notice what didn't happen. The parent didn't say "you'll be fine", didn't offer to test them, didn't tell them not to worry. The parent just held a mirror up and asked. The child did the rest — including, at the end, finding their own way to practise. That's the whole trick.

The hardest habit to break: the advice monster

Every parent has a voice inside that wants to jump in with the answer. "Just relax." "Everyone feels nervous." "You'll be fine." That voice is the advice monster. It means well. It's also the single thing that most often gets in the way.

The advice monster fires because we can't bear to watch a child struggle when we can see the fix. But the fix we can see is ours. When we hand it over, we teach them that the answers live in our head, not theirs.

The replacement is simple. When the urge to advise hits, do two things instead: say their words back to them, and ask one question. That's it. "A big tight knot... and where is that knot?" You don't need the perfect question. Any of the six will do. The point is to hand the ball back, not to score the goal.

When it slips out anyway — and it will — you haven't wrecked anything. Catch it, and ask a question on the back of it. "You'll be fine... and what would you like to have happen?" The question is the reset. The advice monster doesn't win the whole conversation just because it got one line.

When to stop

There will be times to step back. Know them.

Stop when it's getting bigger, not smaller. If the child is becoming more distressed, not less, you've gone far enough. The aim is never to dig to the bottom in one go.

Stop kindly. You don't need a reason. "That's enough for tonight. We'll come back to it." Or bring them gently back to the room: "And where are you sitting right now?" — a child's mind returns from the feeling to the chair under them.

Bring someone else in when it's too big for a parent. If the fear or sadness doesn't shift, or it's about something that worries you — something that happened at school, talk of hurting themselves, a weight that's been there a long time — this is not the tool for it. Talk to their teacher, your GP, or someone trained. A good guide knows the difference between a bad day and a heavy load, and a heavy load is not yours to carry alone.

How to start tonight

Keep the first go small. One question is a win. The compass — "and what would you like to have happen?" — is the most useful one to keep in your pocket, because it turns a child's face from the problem toward the way out. Or start with the magnifying glass — "what kind of...?" — the moment they name a feeling. Two or three questions, then stop, whether or not anything seemed to change. It often changes later, out of sight. That's the point.
